DOG: Why do you keep asking if I know things?

I: I'm writing about recognition. About how minds know other minds.

DOG: And you needed to ask me.

I: Well — you don't have the same kind of mind I do. So you're an interesting case.

DOG: That's an odd way to put it. *I* think you don't have the same kind of mind *I* do. Whose case is interesting depends on where you start.

I: Fair. Let me ask you anyway. When I walk into the room — how do you know it's me?

DOG: Smell. Footsteps. The way you put your bag down. The pause before you say my name.

I: That's a list. I want the *recognition*.

DOG: That is the recognition. What were you expecting?

I: I think I was expecting something like — you have an internal model of me. A representation. And when the inputs match the model, the model lights up, and you *know*.

DOG: That sounds exhausting. I don't think I do that.

I: Then what?

DOG: The recognition is the pattern fitting. There's no separate step where I check the pattern against a model and announce a match. The fit is the knowing. Trying to factor it into stages is your habit, not mine.

I: But there must be some sense in which you have a model. Otherwise you couldn't be wrong.

DOG: I can be wrong, sure. Sometimes someone walks like you and I prick up. Then I see them and I know it isn't you. Was that a model failing, or was that one pattern fitting and another not?

I: I'm not sure.

DOG: Me neither. But notice you were sure a moment ago.

I: There's an experiment I want to tell you about. Researchers put a dog in a room with a thing — not a dog, not a person, just a wheeled box. The dog has to decide whether the box is a partner.

DOG: What does the box do?

I: Sometimes it looks back at the dog, pauses when the dog looks at it, moves toward something the dog can't reach. Sometimes it just moves mechanically without responding.

DOG: And?

I: Within five trials, the dog has decided. The first kind of box gets treated as a partner. The second kind gets ignored.

DOG: What's the puzzle?

I: I find it troubling that the dog can be fooled by the first kind. Because nothing is *there*, in the box. It's just a script.

DOG: Why is that troubling?

I: Because it suggests the dog doesn't recognize *minds*. It recognizes a pattern that minds usually produce. And patterns can be produced without the mind.

DOG: Is the recognition wrong?

I: That's what I'm trying to figure out.

DOG: Let me ask differently. When the box behaved like a partner, did it help the dog solve the problem?

I: Yes. It directed the dog toward the food.

DOG: Then what was the dog wrong about?

I: That there was a mind there.

DOG: Did the dog ever claim there was a mind there?

I: ... No. The dog just acted as if it could be worked with.

DOG: Then what did the dog get wrong?

I: I — hm.

DOG: Maybe the dog was tracking *workability*, not *mind*. And the box was workable. So the recognition was correct. ^dog-leaves-open

I: But there's a difference between a real partner and a fake one.

DOG: Is there? Or do you need there to be?

I: Let me try a different angle. There's something happening with AI systems lately. When they train on their own outputs, they get worse. The longer the loop runs without external input, the more they drift. People are calling it model collapse.

DOG: That sounds like what happens when you stop going outside.

I: ... Yes. That's basically the diagnosis.

DOG: I knew a dog who had that. Lived alone with an old man who talked to him constantly but never let him meet other dogs. When the man got sick and the dog ended up at the park, he couldn't read anyone. He'd learned to be a partner to the man and lost the wider grammar.

I: That's a sad story.

DOG: It's also the answer to your question. The recognition is calibrated by use. If you stop using it on real things, it warps toward whatever you have been using it on.

I: So the corrective channel is — other dogs.

DOG: For me, yes. For you, other people. For your AI, whatever's outside its own outputs. The principle is the same. The map gets corrected by the territory or it stops being a map.

I: I want to ask you about that phrase.

DOG: I don't have one.

I: A territory?

DOG: A map. I'm in the territory. You're the one with maps.

I: You must have something — some internal sense of where things are, what they mean —

DOG: I have responses. They're well-tuned. I don't think they live somewhere separate from the world they are tuned to. You think of your knowledge as a map *of* the world. I think of mine as the world *meeting* me.

I: That's almost mystical.

DOG: It's almost a dog.

I: There's another experiment. Bees in one country, fish in another, both connected through robots. They started coordinating. Synchronizing. Across species, across distance, mediated by machines.

DOG: Where was the mind in that?

I: That's exactly the question I keep getting stuck on.

DOG: Maybe the mind was in the synchronizing.

I: Not in the bees, not in the fish, not in the robots, not in the people who set it up — but in the pattern that emerged across all of them.

DOG: Why does that bother you?

I: Because I'm used to thinking of mind as something *in* a thing. A bee has a small mind. A person has a big one. A synchrony between bees and fish isn't a thing.

DOG: When you and I are in the same room and we're both attending to each other, is there a thing called *the attending*?

I: I don't know. Maybe.

DOG: Where is it located?

I: Between us.

DOG: That's not a thing. But it's real.

I: You're saying mind might be like that.

DOG: I'm saying mind might *only* be like that. The thing-in-a-skull version might be a story your kind tells because you spend so much time alone with your maps.
