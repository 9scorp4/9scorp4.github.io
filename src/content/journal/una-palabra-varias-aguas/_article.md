I have been trying to write about multipolarity for several weeks and the word keeps falling apart in my hands. The trouble is not that I don't understand what people mean when they use it. The trouble is that they mean different things, and the word does not flag the difference.

In the version most current in Western analysis — think tank papers, op-eds in the *Financial Times*, talks given by people with security clearances — multipolarity is a topology. The world used to have one center; now it has several. The work of the analyst is to identify the new poles, locate their boundaries, draw the spheres of influence, and ask which existing arrangements survive the reconfiguration. The frame is structural and the interest is in shape.

In the version most current in [[journal:al-borde-del-fenomeno#^global-south-channels|Global South writing]] — academic, journalistic, often produced by people who have read the same theory but apply it from the other side of the asymmetry — multipolarity is an opportunity. The argument is that smaller and middle-sized countries, having spent the post-Cold-War decades operating under effectively unipolar conditions, now have more partners to deal with. They can hedge. They can play powers against each other. They can negotiate terms that were not negotiable when there was only one set of terms to take. The frame is practical and the interest is in maneuvering.

These are not competing definitions of the same thing. They are descriptions of different phenomena that happen to share a name. The Western analyst is asking *what shape is the system*. The Global South writer is asking *what can I do inside it*. Almost a figure/ground inversion, with one word pulling double duty.

---

The complication that interests me sits in a parenthetical that shows up, in some form, at the end of most Global South multipolarity arguments. The hedging story goes: smaller countries can now diversify their dependencies — *as long as Russia, Iran, and China allow them to*. Often the line is phrased more generously. Sometimes it is implied rather than written. But it is almost always there, because the writer is honest enough to know that the new freedom is not unconditional.

That parenthetical does serious work. It collapses what came before it. If the new freedom is granted by the new poles, on their terms, with their ability to withdraw it if the maneuvering exceeds what they will tolerate, then the structural relation of dependency has not changed. What has changed is the number of vendors. The Global South can shop, but the shop is owned by the same kind of entity that owned the previous shop. The configuration is multipolar; the dependency is preserved. ^permission-not-freedom

This is the shape Bateson would have called a [[journal:lo-que-vestia-el-poema#:~:text=The%20frame%20names%20a%20transaction%20that%20cannot%20occur|double bind]], if applied at the right level. The new arrangement appears to offer freedom at the level of the move (more partners, more options) while *requiring* unfreedom at the level above (the move is granted, not taken). What the freedom is freedom *of* is determined by the parties whose permission constitutes it. The escape from unipolar dependence is a contract with the new poles to recognize the new poles. The shape preserves at one level what it appears to change at another.

I am not saying the new arrangement is not preferable to the old one. It often is, for the actors involved. More vendors means worse terms for the vendors and better terms for the buyers, up to a point — which is the point at which the vendors decide collectively, formally or informally, what their floor will be. The Global South gain is real and the constraint is real. Both can be true.

What I am saying is that the word *multipolarity* makes it hard to hold both at once. The word is set up to describe a topology, and topologies are flat. The word does not have a way to register that the configuration it names *also names* the permission structure that sustains it.

---

Korzybski's old line — the map is not the territory — has gotten a lot of use in the garden, [[journal:lo-que-corrige-el-mapa#:~:text=The%20map%20needs%20the%20territory|paid for in territory]] elsewhere. I want to push it in a slightly different direction here. The Western reading of multipolarity is a cartographer's reading. It treats the configuration as a map to be drawn — poles to be located, boundaries to be plotted, the shape of the new system to be made legible. The Global South reading, in its most honest moments, is a navigator's reading. It treats the configuration as waters to be crossed. The interest is not in the shape of the system but in the channels it permits, the customs offices, the fuel prices, the questions asked at the ports. *Which doors open at which hours.*

These are not better or worse maps of the same thing. They are different relations to the phenomenon. The cartographer is at the level where the configuration is the object. The navigator is at the level where the configuration is the medium. The cartographer wants to know what *multipolarity is*. The navigator wants to know what *passes through it*. ^object-vs-medium

A small consequence worth naming: the cartographer's interest is information-poor in a specific way that the navigator's is not. The unmarked center of a system does not have to distinguish itself from anything beyond it. Everything outside is *abroad*, *the rest*, *the world*. A single coarse distinction. The navigator, by virtue of being inside the channels, has to register many more distinctions: which port asks which questions, which flags rise and when, which radio the harbor master is listening to, which paper opens which door. The edge has more distinguishable states because it has to live among them. ^edge-information

---

This sets up a paradox I am still trying to think clearly. The edge has epistemic privilege. The cartographer at the center is operating with a coarser map because the center never had to draw a finer one. The navigator at the edge has a finer practical knowledge because nothing about staying alive at the edge permits coarseness.

But the edge has structural subordination. The navigator can see more than the cartographer and act on less. The differences they perceive are differences they perceive *within* a configuration whose shape is maintained by parties who do not need to perceive them. Bateson defined *information* as [[journal:lo-que-corrige-el-mapa#:~:text=the%20difference%20that%20makes%20a%20difference|a difference that makes a difference]]. He was assuming, more or less, that the observer who perceived the difference could also be the one who acted on it. The navigator's situation breaks that assumption. The differences perceived at the edge are mostly unactionable at the level at which they are perceived. You can register every gradient between Beijing's interest and Moscow's interest. You cannot redirect the gradient.

What is an observer who can perceive but not act? Not nothing. Not a victim. Something more like a witness whose witnessing is genuinely valuable — and may, at some level, be *part of what the configuration requires*. This is [[journal:smash-laterally-i#^cost-relocation|the lateral move at geopolitical scale]]: the cost is not eliminated but relocated, from the pole to the edge. The poles need the edge to be perceptive. The edge interprets signals the poles cannot send to each other directly. It absorbs variety the poles cannot absorb themselves. It does work for the configuration that the configuration cannot do without it. This is not quite [[journal:smash-laterally-iii#^requisite-variety|Beer's variety equation]], but it rhymes with it: the new arrangement may need the edge's perceptiveness in order to function at all, while structurally requiring that the edge not become a pole. ^variety-rhyme

The disturbing version of this thought is that the edge's epistemic privilege might be *constitutive* of the multipolar configuration rather than a transitional state on the way to something else. The configuration may require the edge to keep seeing finely without ever becoming a pole. The optimistic version is that epistemic privilege eventually translates into structural change — that finer perception accumulates into different forms of organization, alliances, infrastructures, that eventually remake what the poles can do. I don't know which is closer to true. I don't think anyone does. The question may not have a fact-of-the-matter, only a trajectory that gets settled by what the actors involved do over time.

---

There is a recursion I should name before closing. The framing I have just offered — *epistemic privilege at the edge, structural pinning at the same edge* — is itself an edge-actor framing. It is the kind of framing that becomes available, and rhetorically useful, *to people positioned at the edge*. "We are not hedging, we are perceiving." This does not make the framing wrong. It does mean the framing is also a move. It can be both honest description and useful self-legitimation at once, and the writer who deploys it should know that they are doing both.

The garden is generally hospitable to this kind of loop — [[journal:smash-laterally-iii#^inside-empire|the observer being part of the observation]] — and I am content to leave it as a loop rather than try to step outside it. The step outside would itself be a move on the same board.

---

What I have come to about the word *multipolarity*, then, is that it cannot be used cleanly. It is two phenomena pretending to be one. From the cartographer's level, the word names a topology — a shape of poles and spheres, drawn at a level the cartographer is not actually at. From the navigator's level, the word does not name; it indexes practical conditions of crossing, which is a different operation than naming. The cartographer's *multipolarity* and the navigator's *multipolarity* are connected by the fact that the same waters are involved. They are not connected by a shared concept. ^two-phenomena-one-word

A writer who wants to use the word honestly has, I think, three options. Use it as a cartographer's term and accept that the use will systematically mis-describe what the navigators are experiencing. Use it as a navigator's term and accept that the use will sound to cartographers like a refusal to think systemically. Or — what this piece has tried to do — use it as a *figure of the level-confusion*, an example of the kind of word that breaks under cross-position reading, and try to keep both readings in the frame at the same time by not pretending the word resolves them.

The fourth option, of course, is to stop using the word and use other words. I don't think that is available. *Multipolarity* is the word in circulation; the conversation is happening under it. The work is to keep using it while flagging, as often as the writing can stand, that the word is doing more than one thing and that the appearance of unity is a feature of the word, not of the phenomenon.

---

A last note. Most of what I have written here is from the cartographer's level — descriptions of structures, frames, level-confusions. This is the level a cuaderno is at. The navigator's level is harder to write *to* from a cuaderno, because the cuaderno is the cartographer's instrument. The metalogue that accompanies this entry tries, partly, to register what the cuaderno cannot — by being itself a conversation at port between someone with a notebook and someone with a folded paper, neither of whom can fully use the other's document. Both at the same port. Both with different work to do.
