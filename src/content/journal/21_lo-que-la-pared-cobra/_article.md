The box on the desk holds three billion parameters and not one more. I have been looking at it the way you look at something you have already decided to buy and have not yet paid for. Next to it, in a browser tab, sits the order page for a machine that would hold more — more money, more configuration, a standing obligation to keep it current. And underneath both is a question I had been treating as if it were about hardware, which it is not: what does it cost to not depend?

The honest first answer is that the question is badly formed, because it assumes sovereignty is something you buy. It is not a purchase. It is a rate. ^rate-not-sum

The event that made the threat legible was not abstract. In June a government directive ordered a frontier lab to cut off access to its two most capable models for any foreign national, and [the lab complied by going dark for everyone](https://www.anthropic.com/news/fable-mythos-access), because it could not partition its users finely enough to do anything less. Sessions ended in errors. Queries routed down to older, weaker models. The capability did not degrade; it disappeared, overnight, by fiat, for people who had built on it and had no other host on earth that held those weights. If you are not a US national building on US-hosted models — *si no sos de acá, construyendo sobre lo de acá* — that directive was a letter with your name partway on it.

---

So you start mapping exits, and they sort themselves by which layer you agree to keep renting. Rent the frontier and you rent everything: maximal capability, zero ownership, a perpetual bill, a kill switch in someone else's hand. [[cultivation:crisol|Distil a small model of your own]] and you convert that perpetual bill into a capital asset you hold outright — but the asset is yours to depreciate, and it was *made of* the frontier's output, so the dependency did not vanish, it moved to the moment of genesis. Rent a server to run open weights and you own the capability while renting only the compute, which is the layer where rental is cheap and eviction is survivable, because the same weights come up on another host in an afternoon. Own the metal entirely and you have converted all the flow into a single stock, plus electricity, plus the fact that you are now, and from here on, the sysadmin.

Four configurations, one structure. None of them removes the cost. Each one only chooses where to put it.

---

The question on the desk is the smallest version of one the garden has been circling at much larger scales — [[journal:al-borde-del-fenomeno#^three-scales-invariant|the same shape repeating]] from the geopolitical down through the infrastructural to the cognitive, the sovereignty move always being production at the edge with the conditions of negotiation kept in your own hands, the colonial move always being their enclosure into a flow you only consume. The June directive added a fresh, brutal instance at the top of that ladder. It is not the only answer being given right now, and *multipolar* is just the name for the fact that there is more than one.

The directive is one answer: treat the model as a munition, control it at the layer of access, decide who may think with it. A second power has been answering the opposite way — policing not who may reach the model but what the model may do to a job. Courts there have [ruled that replacing a worker with AI is not lawful grounds for dismissal](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-02/chinese-court-rules-firms-can-t-lay-off-workers-on-ai-grounds), and the same policy turn has begun to organise a new unit of economic life: the *one-person company*, a single human running a firm staffed by AI — the human keeps strategy, judgment, the decision, and the machine absorbs the operational work beneath. If the division sounds familiar it should, because it is exactly what you are doing at the desk: keep the metasystem, delegate the operations. The only difference is that there a state is organising the individual-as-viable-system at scale, and here you are improvising it alone, against the grain.

And the trap repeats unchanged at the smallest scale. The one-person sovereign owns the metasystem and rents the substrate — the compute, the data, the models, all provisioned by the state that blessed the arrangement — so the switch did not vanish, *cambió de bandera*. A super-individual on rented state infrastructure and you on a rented GPU are holding the same shape from opposite ends of the earth, and both have answered *where does the switch sit* with *somewhere I do not fully hold.* ^opposite-ends

More poles is more vendors, not more freedom — [[journal:una-palabra-varias-aguas#^permission-not-freedom|the configuration stays multipolar and the dependency stays preserved]] — and freedom that is granted can be withdrawn. Multipolarity is not more room to stand. It is rival owners of the same switch.

---

This is the thing the hardware question hides. Sovereignty never subtracts cost; it transmutes it into a form you have to keep paying. It converts flow into stock, and now you hold the depreciating asset — the renter outsources obsolescence to the vendor, and that outsourcing is most of what the subscription quietly buys. It converts suffered risk into worked risk, which is the polite name for labour: the price of being able to fix it yourself is the standing obligation to fix it yourself, *para siempre* — Illich's bargain, in which the convivial tool hands you control and control turns out to be inseparable from its upkeep. It imposes a capability ceiling, and this is the cruel term, because the best tool you can hold is structurally never the best tool that exists; the frontier is precisely the thing that requires the concentration you are leaving. The open model you would run is [near-frontier this year](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/gemma-4/) and a curiosity in three, and the gap widens fastest exactly when the field moves fastest, which is now, and the decay is now yours to hold.

And it relocates dependency rather than abolishing it. Own the inference, you rent the lineage. Own the weights, you rent the GPU. Own the GPU, you depend on the foundry, the grid, the continued willingness of open labs to release at all. There is no terminal sovereignty. There is only a residual — and the residual is where the next switch hides.

Which is why the exit keeps getting billed in the currency of the thing you are leaving. To build the off-ramp you pay tolls to the road. *No hay salida que no pase por lo que dejás* — and that is not a trap so much as the plain topology of leaving an infrastructure you are inside of. The cost of refusal is real, and it is denominated, every time, in the units of what you refuse.

---

The precise word for what this is comes from the library, and it is not decoration. Maturana's autopoiesis: a living system is not a thing you possess but a process that holds its own boundary by continuously producing itself, and the production costs energy without pause. Stop, and the boundary dissolves back into the environment that was always pressing on it. Technological sovereignty has exactly that signature. It is not a fortress you build once and then hold. It is a membrane you metabolise. ^membrane-not-fortress

Let the box age, let your own competence lapse, let the open lineage you depend on get quietly captured upstream, and the sovereignty evaporates without anyone needing to revoke a thing. The cost is a rate, not a sum, because the wall is alive, and alive means it is being paid for or it is dissolving. This is as true of a state's membrane as of the one on your desk — which is why, once you have seen the shape, the geopolitics and the box stop being separate subjects.

Here I have to disarm a sentence before it does damage, because in the conversation that became this piece the machine said it flat — *no hay afuera* — and said that way it frightens, and it should, because it is darker than the argument. There is no *free* outside, no place you reach and then rest. But "no free exit" is not "no exit." The outside is not a location. It is the cut. It is the off-switch you actually hold, the refusal a system can meter but cannot prevent. Spencer-Brown's mark: the distinction is not found, it is *drawn*, and only something with a stake in the difference can draw it. ^the-outside-is-the-cut

---

And then the term that keeps all of this from curdling into a sermon. Sovereignty is insurance, and insurance priced against a threat you do not face is just a tax you volunteered to pay. The directive made one threat model legible and rational to insure against; it did not make sovereignty a virtue. It is the easiest thing in the world to fetishise — to chase as identity, as proof of seriousness, as a clean refusal — and mispriced it becomes the most expensive purchase on the menu: you pay the full metabolic rate, the depreciation and the labour and the widening gap and the vigilance, to insure against something that may never come, while the frontier pulls away from you the whole time you congratulate yourself for leaving it.

So the last cost is the one easiest to miss. The cost of sovereignty includes the cost of being wrong about needing it. ^cost-of-being-wrong

Price it against the threat. Never against the self-image. The box holds three billion parameters and not one more, and whether that ceiling is a wall worth metabolising or a comfortable story about independence depends entirely on whether the letter really had your name on it. *Y eso* — the reading of the threat, the drawing of the line, the decision to pay the rate or refuse it — no lo puede hacer la máquina. Es tarea de uno.
