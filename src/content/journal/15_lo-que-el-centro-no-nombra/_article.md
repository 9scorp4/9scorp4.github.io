In the spring of 1789, the Estates-General convened at Versailles. By June they had renamed themselves the National Assembly. The room they met in was not designed for what they were doing — a long hall, the president's chair at one end, benches arrayed before it. Delegates began to seat themselves by affinity: monarchists and clergy on the president's right, the partisans of reform on the left. The arrangement persisted through successive assemblies. Within a generation, *droite* and *gauche* — and *derecha* and *izquierda*, and *right* and *left* — [had become the names of political tendencies in themselves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left%E2%80%93right_political_spectrum), no longer requiring the room they came from.

The chair is the part of this story that gets forgotten.

The chair was the position from which the distinction was drawn. Left and right made sense only in relation to it. To be on the left was to be on the president's left; to be on the right was to be on the president's right. The chair itself was not on the axis it organized. It was the *condition* of the axis — the point from which the room could be divided in two and the two sides could appear to be exhaustive. Move the chair, and left and right become different things. Remove the chair, and the axis ceases to be drawable at all.

We have inherited the spatial metaphor. We have not inherited the chair.

---

What I want to call the [form of the distinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laws_of_Form) is this: every binary political axis depends on an unmarked position that draws the distinction without appearing as one of its terms. The unmarked position presents itself as outside the axis, neutral, descriptive. It is the position from which left and right can be seen *as* left and right. To occupy it is to occupy a view that calls itself a view from nowhere.

The trick is structural. The center is not a position between left and right. The center is the line itself, posing as one of its sides. When someone describes themselves as a centrist, they are not claiming to hold positions between two extremes — they are claiming to occupy the position from which the extremes can be recognized as extreme. The claim is not about content; it is about altitude. Centrism is the politics of altitude.

This is why centrism, in nearly every political landscape, carries the rhetorical charge of *non-ideology*. The left has positions. The right has positions. The center merely sees. The asymmetry — marked terms against an unmarked condition — does massive ideological work while presenting itself as the absence of ideology. It is how power describes its own location as natural.

The left/right binary itself is not the deep problem. The deep problem is the apparent *line* on which left and right can be arranged, and the chair from which the line is drawn. The line carries the claim that all political positions can be made commensurable — placed in a single ordering — which is a substantive ontological commitment dressed up as a spatial convenience. The chair carries the claim that there is a place to stand from which the ordering can be performed neutrally. Both are wrong. Both are, in the moment of speaking, inescapable.

---

The metaphor migrates across landscapes that have nothing else in common.

In the United States, the gravitational center of the spectrum sits well to the right of what counts as center-left in most of Western Europe. The same substantive policy positions — universal healthcare, public transit funding, parental leave — register as *progressive* in one frame and as *moderate* in the other. The label does the work the policy does not. The chair has moved; the axis has been redrawn around it; what was on the left in one room is in the center in another.

In contemporary China, a single-party state describes itself internally in terms of a left/right/center distribution: the Maoist left, the reformist right, the synthesizing center held by the current general secretary. The frame operates *inside* what an electoral-democratic frame would call a single position. The room has nested; the line has recurred at a smaller scale; the same operation runs at a different level. Wherever there is differentiation, there is an axis. Wherever there is an axis, there is a chair.

The case that makes the operation most visible is Iran. The internal political vocabulary is *eslāhtalab* and *osulgara* — reformist and principlist. Western journalism translates these as *moderate* and *hardliner*, which preserves the binary while changing what it indexes. Around the binary, the Supreme Leader and [the Guardian Council](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Council-of-Guardians) vet candidates, define which variation counts as legitimate, organize the field on which the eslāhtalab/osulgara contest is allowed to occur. The constitution avows this — [*velayat-e faqih*](https://www.britannica.com/topic/velayat-e-faqih), the guardianship of the jurist — as the theological-political ground from which the political field is structured. The chair has a name. The chair has an address. The chair is constitutionally declared to be the chair.

In Western liberal democracies the analogous function is also performed — distributed across donor systems, primary mechanisms, security agencies, the boundaries of media access — but it is performed implicitly and deniably. The chair is everywhere and nowhere; the field is organized without anyone needing to say from where. This is not less ideological than the Iranian arrangement. It is more sophisticated, in the sense that it is harder to contest. A center that disclaims being a center is more durable than a center that announces itself. The avowal in Tehran throws the disclaimer in Washington into relief.

Colombia is the case I want to write about next — where mutual accusation between left and right around drug trafficking and political violence has, since the 1980s, run a particular variation of this form, with a particular brutality. I will hold that here. The point now is that the form recurs. It is not a feature of any single landscape. It is a technology of political self-organization that any sufficiently differentiated political body can deploy at any scale, in any vocabulary.

---

Here is where the argument turns, because the form has a second-order property that the spatial metaphor hides.

The axes that become load-bearing in a political landscape are not only generated from within. They are also *elicited* by what wants to be able to read the landscape from outside. Iran has many live political axes — clerical/lay, IRGC/civilian administration, bazaari/state-employee, rural/urban, ethnic Persian/Azeri/Kurd/Baloch. *Moderate/hardliner* is the one that travels into Western coverage, because it is the axis legible from outside the system. *Moderate* means willing to deal with us. *Hardliner* means won't. The frame doesn't only describe Iran. It describes what an outside observer needs Iran to be in order to engage with it. The axis is co-produced by what is there and by what is being asked.

This was true of the Colombian left/right binary too. It was internally generated — there is real history, real organizing, real ideological content — and it was a projection of Cold War cartography, the global anti-communist frame needing local manifestations to justify itself. The two reinforced each other; you cannot separate the frame *they have* from the frame *we needed them to have*. The observer is in the room. The chair I have been describing is not only inside the system being observed. The chair is partly the position of the observer who needs the system to be legible in their terms.

This is the move Bateson points at, almost in passing, when he names information as [*a difference that makes a difference*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information#%22A_difference_that_makes_a_difference%22). The phrase usually gets cited for its first half. The second half is where the load is: *to whom*. A difference is a difference *for some observer*. The differentiator's needs shape which differences get selected as load-bearing. Frames are not just deployed by the differentiated. They are elicited by what wants to be able to read. [[journal:lo-que-corrige-el-mapa|The channels through which minds recognize each other]] are also the channels through which what counts as a mind gets decided.

What this implies for our own position — yours, mine, reading this — is that there is no clean place to stand from which the chair can be analyzed without becoming one. To name the form of the distinction from outside is to occupy the very unmarked position the analysis is supposed to expose. The view from nowhere reborn as methodology. The chair migrates upward; the same operation runs at the meta-level.

There is no escape that does not enact the trap.

---

What I want to land on is not a way out. The argument does not produce a position. It produces, at best, a different *quality of relation* to the position one is already in.

Naming the frame is already inside the frame. The pretense that one can name it from outside is the same unmarked-center operation, ported up a level. The analytical and the practical cannot be separated — the analyst who pretends to stand outside is acting (in a particular way, with particular consequences, including the consequence of disowning the act); the actor who refuses analysis is analyzing (with the analysis foreclosed from revision, which is its own commitment). Both at once isn't a synthesis or a balance. It is the recognition that the separation was a category error from the start.

What is available is something closer to what Bateson called [Learning III](http://www.biolinguagem.com/ling_cog_cult/bateson_1972_%20logicalcategories_learningcommunication.pdf). Not a step outside the frame — that step is exactly the unmarked-center move — but the capacity to hold the frame as one possible move while making moves. Which only happens *in* making moves.

The honest version is to act without the alibi. To use the categories one inherits — left, right, center, moderate, hardliner, reformist — knowing they are doing work one cannot fully control, knowing they are organized around chairs one cannot fully see, knowing one's own position as observer is in the room they organize. This is not a defeat. It is the recognition that there is no exterior from which the work could be done cleanly, and that pretending there is is itself the operation the work was supposed to interrupt.

The chair was the part of the 1789 story that got forgotten. The forgetting was not an oversight. The forgetting is how the chair stays.
