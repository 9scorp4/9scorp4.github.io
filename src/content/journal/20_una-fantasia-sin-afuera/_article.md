The phrase *AI will replace jobs* smuggles three distinct claims into one shape. They live at different scales and ask for different responses.

The first is *task-level automation*: specific operations inside a job get done by software. This is real, ongoing, and as a phenomenon largely uncontroversial — it has been happening since the Jacquard loom. The second is *role-level substitution*: entire job categories collapse because their bundle of tasks gets fully automated. This happens, more slowly than predicted, with severe transition costs systematically externalized onto the workers being displaced. The third is the civilizational claim — *mass labor replacement* — that human labor as such can be removed from the production of value.

Most discourse about AI and jobs equivocates among the three. The forecasts that announce "X percent of jobs replaced by year Y" usually mean the first read as the second. The post-labor rhetoric in venture-capital register — gestures at universal basic income, talk of a post-work economy, the standard founder-discourse about the end of work — is the third, projected from a position where the first looks like a frictionless upgrade. The fantasy register lives at the third scale, and the fantasy is incoherent on its own terms. ^three-scales

It is also a fantasy held from a specific position, which is part of why it persists in the face of being incoherent. The position critique runs alongside the structural one throughout what follows. Both are needed because either alone leaves something unexplained.

---

The political-economic case begins with Karl Polanyi. In *The Great Transformation*, labor — alongside land and money — is named as a *fictitious commodity*: not produced for sale, not separable from the life of the person bearing it. Treating it as a real commodity, fully substitutable and tradable, tears the social fabric in which labor is embedded. Society then mobilizes counter-movements to defend itself; Polanyi traced the double movement through nineteenth-century industrialization. The proposal to remove labor from the production of value is the same move at higher voltage — an attempt to complete the commodification project by removing the awkward thing being commodified.

The position note that attaches to Polanyi is that the disembedded view is what makes the move look reasonable in the first place. From outside the social fabric in which labor lives, labor appears as an input — replaceable, optimizable, contingent. From inside, it appears as the substance of a life. The fantasy is sustained by holding the disembedded view as if it were the only view, by a seat for which [[journal:lo-que-no-cruza#^forbidden-engine|the inside has never been visible]]. ^disembedded-view

Marx supplies the internal contradiction the boosters do not usually answer, and he supplies it twice — at the level of the firm and at the level of cognition. In the labor theory of value, surplus arises from living labor; constant capital — machines, by extension AI — only transfers value, it does not create it. Push the ratio of constant to variable capital toward its limit and you eliminate the source of profit itself. Marx named this the tendency of the rate of profit to fall; the mechanism is structural. The more capital saves on labor, the less labor remains to extract surplus from. Full automation is, in this register, the form of capital's self-cancellation. The dynamic — systems that require growth consume their own conditions of growth — is observable elsewhere, and one need not accept the labor theory in its strict form to recognize it.

The sharper version appears in the *Grundrisse*, in the so-called *fragment on machines*. Marx anticipated — in 1858 — the moment when general social knowledge would be absorbed into fixed capital directly, when the worker would stand alongside the production process rather than at its center, as its watchman and regulator. The substantive content of production would no longer be the worker's own labor but the accumulated cognitive labor of generations, congealed into the machine. Marx took this to mean that labor-time would cease to be the measure of value, and that capital would face a crisis it could not metabolize. AI is the fragment on machines arriving at the cognitive layer. The same prediction Marx named as a structural crisis is now being run as a feature. ^fragment-on-machines

The position note that attaches to Marx is that the contradiction is invisible from the seat because the seat's self-conception depends on its invisibility. Capital that recognized itself as living off labor could not propose its own removal of labor as liberation. The founder-investor experiences himself as the locus of value creation; the labor theory of value names him as the appropriator of value created elsewhere. Acknowledging the mechanism would dissolve the identity, so the mechanism stays unacknowledged, and the contradiction it points at stays invisible. The fantasy assumes capital can survive removing the thing that constitutes it as capital — and the assumption is durable because the seat from which it is held is precisely the seat for which capital's parasitism cannot register as parasitism.

John Maynard Keynes is the honest version of the post-work imaginary, and the most instructive failure. In a 1930 essay he argued that productivity gains would deliver a fifteen-hour week by 2030, with the main human problem becoming what to do with leisure. He was right about the productivity and wrong about everything else. Gains went to more production, not less work; wants did not saturate, capitalism kept manufacturing new ones; and political power followed capital rather than labor. The Keynesian post-labor scenario assumed a level of distributional politics that the post-1970s settlement deliberately dismantled. When the present discourse gestures at universal basic income, the question is not *can it be done in principle* — Keynes already answered yes. The question is what political coalition forces it, and how, once labor has lost its structural leverage. The honest historical answer is that it does not happen on its own.

David Graeber sharpens this further. *Bullshit Jobs* documented that significant chunks of existing employment are economically pointless and known to be so by the people doing them, retained because the system uses employment as its distribution mechanism for income and its discipline mechanism for the population. The AI-replaces-labor story assumes the bottleneck is technical capability. Graeber's point is that the bottleneck has been political-distributional for decades; the automation slack already exists. So when AI is pitched as the thing that finally forces the post-work conversation, it is worth asking who benefits from that pitch, and why now, and what it routes attention away from. The bullshit jobs were always optional.

The position note that attaches to Graeber is that the existence of these jobs is visible from below — the people in them know — and structurally invisible from above. The seat that proposes to automate work has, by the design of the seat, no view onto which work was being done and which work was being performed. From the seat, all of it appears as output, and all output is in principle automatable.

---

So far the argument has stayed in political economy. What follows changes altitude. The fantasy is incoherent at the level of the economy, and it is also incoherent at the level of cognition — and the cognitive register is harder to see because it has fewer constituencies organized to defend against it.

Bernard Stiegler is the most useful guide here. His concept of *proletarianization* generalizes Marx's: a worker is proletarianized when the knowledge of their craft is absorbed into the tool, leaving them as an executor rather than a knower. The classic case is the deskilling of artisans by industrial machinery. Stiegler extended this to *cognitive* proletarianization — when memory, attention, judgment, savoir-vivre are externalized into digital systems, the user is left with the gesture of cognition without its substance. Large language models are the apotheosis: they absorb not just procedural skill but the texture of thought, leaving the user with the prompt and the accept button. This is what *replacing the job* actually means in cognitive work. Not the elimination of the worker but their reduction to a verifier of outputs they could not have produced and increasingly cannot evaluate. ^cognitive-proletarianization

The position note that attaches to Stiegler is the sharp one. The seat proposing automation does not feel deskilled because the seat was never doing the skill. From inside the founder-investor position, the labor being automated has always appeared as a flow of outputs; the seat has never been inside the process that produced them. So when the seat proposes to automate the process, it experiences the proposal as efficient rather than as evacuating. The cognitive proletarianization is invisible from the seat because the cognition being proletarianized is not the seat's. It belongs to the person whose work is being described, from outside, as input.

Gregory Bateson lets us see why this matters beyond the individual. *An idea is an idea by virtue of its differences from other ideas.* Cognition, at the population level, depends on variation — distributed, error-prone, contextual variation. [[journal:lo-que-corrige-el-mapa#^corrective-channel-definition|The corrective capacity]] of a culture lives in that variation: enough independent minds noticing different things that the system can update against itself. When cognition gets routed through a small number of models trained on a snapshot of past text, variation collapses in exactly the dimension that produces correction. Monoculture in the noosphere. A society that has automated its thinking has not become more efficient at thinking; it has lost [[journal:lo-que-corrige-el-mapa#^model-collapse-bateson|the distributed substrate that *was* its thinking]]. ^monoculture-noosphere

Ivan Illich names the practical mechanism by which automation generates work it cannot count. Every labor-saving tool produces *shadow work*: the unpaid labor of integration, maintenance, learning, and especially of compensating for what the tool does badly. Cars saved transport labor and produced the shadow work of car-ownership — insurance, parking, repair, the unpaid driving itself. AI is generating its shadow work at speed: prompt-crafting, output-verification, hallucination-correction, integration into workflows not designed for probabilistic outputs, constant re-skilling as the models change. The *AI replaces the job* framing makes this invisible because shadow work is not counted as work. It just lives in the worker's evenings.

The position note that attaches to Illich is that shadow work is invisible by definition from the benefiting side. The seat that gets the labor-saving never sees the unpaid labor that emerges to compensate. From the seat, the tool simply works.

Hannah Arendt sits where the two registers meet, and her diagnosis is older than the current moment but sharper than most of what has been written about it since. In *The Human Condition* she distinguished labor — the biological necessity of reproducing life — from work — the fabrication of a durable world of objects — from action, political speech and deed, only possible among plural others. Her worry, in 1958, was that modernity was producing a *society of laborers* in which work and action had already been hollowed out — and that automating labor in *that* condition would leave humans without any of the three. No necessity to organize around, no durable world to make, no polis to act within. She called this *a society of laborers without labor* and described it as the worst-case outcome of automation, not the best. The current fantasy proposes exactly this and calls it liberation. The Arendtian objection is not that we would be poor — basic income handles that — but that the conditions for being political beings together would be gone. ^laborers-without-labor

---

The danger is downstream of the impossibility — specifically, of trying to do the impossible thing anyway. By the argument given, mass labor replacement at the civilizational scale cannot succeed. What can happen is the attempt: displacement faster than absorption mechanisms can form, hollowing-out of cognitive substrate before its loss is visible, concentration of surviving leverage in a small number of hands. The fantasy does not have to be coherent to be destructive. The enclosures were not coherent either; they produced the modern world anyway.

What makes the attempt structurally robust is that the people in a position to make it are the people structurally insulated from what would falsify it. The fantasy is held from a specific seat — venture capital, founder-investor, capital allocator — and [[journal:lo-que-no-cruza#^forbidden-engine|that seat has been organized, by its own logic, to not see the things the critique points at]]. The embedded character of labor, the political-distributional bottleneck, the cognitive substance of work, the variation that constitutes thinking, the shadow work generated by labor-saving, the conditions for being political beings together — none of these are visible from the seat. Not because the people in the seat are stupid or cruel, but because the seat itself is constituted by not seeing them. ^seat-not-seeing

Polanyi would have recognized the move immediately. Disembedding always looks reasonable from inside the disembedded view. The fantasy reproduces itself robustly because the position from which it is held is precisely the position from which what would refute it cannot appear.

The work, then, is not to convince the seat. The seat cannot be convinced by an argument it cannot hear. The work is to keep visible — for everyone who is not in the seat — what is being proposed, what cannot succeed, what can happen anyway, and what would be lost in the attempt.
