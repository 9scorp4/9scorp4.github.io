I heard the story on an Instagram reel. Mothers in Africa, the seventies, free samples of formula. By the time the samples ran out, the milk had stopped coming. The mothers bought more formula, mixed it with water that wasn't safe, and the babies died. The reel said ten million children. I went looking, because the number was the kind of number you go looking about.

The number turned out to be approximately correct, in a register the reel didn't quite have. A [peer-reviewed estimate](https://www.nber.org/papers/w24452) by Paul Gertler and colleagues, working with data from 2.48 million births across 46 countries, put the figure at about 10.9 million excess infant deaths between 1960 and 2015 across low- and middle-income countries. Not Africa alone. Not the seventies alone. The pattern accumulated across five decades and three continents, with a peak around 1981 when an estimated 212,000 infants died per year among mothers without clean water access. The reel compressed the scale; it did not invent it.

The mechanism turned out to be more precise than the reel had room to say. Lactation is a responsive system. Milk supply depends on continued stimulation by the infant — the breast that isn't nursed won't keep producing. Once a mother was convinced to supplement with formula, her supply genuinely declined, and the decline locked her into formula dependency at exactly the moment the free samples ran out. Nestlé's ["milk nurses"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1977_Nestl%C3%A9_boycott) — sales representatives dressed as nurses, deployed in maternity wards — distributed samples timed to outlast the mother's lactation. This wasn't deception about a static product. This was the deliberate capture of a biological feedback loop.

That's the part I want to hold onto, because the rest of the argument lives in it.

---

What Nestlé did was not displace a thing with another thing. Milk isn't a thing the mother has. Milk is a process she is engaged in with the infant. It exists in the relational loop between them — stimulus, response, supply, demand. Remove the loop for long enough and the capacity atrophies. The commodity (formula) didn't merely substitute for the output (milk). It dissolved the substrate that produced the output. That's the structural cruelty of the case. Not selling a bad product, but selling a product whose adoption destroyed the unpriced commons the product was replacing.

State the operation cleanly: select a relational system; insert a commodity at the interface between its parts; watch the substrate atrophy; sell the commodity to the people whose substrate has dissolved. [[journal:lo-que-el-espejo-no-corrige#^cost-of-refusal|What's missing is the stake]] — and a commodity, by definition, stakes nothing. ^commodification-operation

This is the template. It applies anywhere a capacity lives in relational substrate rather than in individual property. Identity exists in community recognition; commodified as personal brand, it requires platform mediation and the recognition substrate atrophies. Relationships exist in mutuality; commodified through dating apps or paid-friendship platforms, the capacity to form unmediated bonds attenuates. Sex exists in encounter or intimacy; industrialized through porn and platforms, the relational competence around sex declines in measurable ways across populations. In each case the commodity doesn't merely substitute. It displaces, and in displacing, dissolves the substrate that produced what was being sold.

This is the [[journal:al-borde-del-fenomeno#:~:text=the%20configuration%20question|configuration question]] under another name. What's the medium of selection. Who owns it. Each commodification answers those questions in a particular way — by inserting a metered, owned, atomized object at the interface where the relational substrate used to do the work.

---

Now the harder case. At the [BlackRock Infrastructure Summit](https://www.rev.com/transcripts/altman-speaks-at-blackrocks-us-infrastructure-summit), Sam Altman said: *we see a future where intelligence is a utility like electricity or water, and people buy it on demand to do whatever they want*. The phrasing was unusually clean. It echoes the [1950s promise](https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/basic-ref/students/history-101/too-cheap-to-meter) that nuclear power would be too cheap to meter — a promise that never materialized but did motivate enormous capital allocation in the meantime.

What does it mean to commodify intelligence. The proposal is to take cognition — pattern-matched well enough by sufficiently large models — and sell it metered, tiered, segmented. Slow cheap thinking for background tasks. Fast premium thinking for real-time interaction. Pricing differentiated by use. Markets organizing access. Capital determining who gets the deeper reasoning, the larger context window, the more persistent memory.

The Nestlé case offers a structural prediction: the commodity will not merely substitute for the cognitive substrate. It will dissolve it.

Four features make this case harder than the formula case, not easier.

First, intelligence is already relational, in a sense more rigorous than metaphor. The position the corpus has been holding in absentia — Bateson's — is that mind is not located in the head. [[journal:lo-que-corrige-el-mapa#^mind-as-relational-pattern|Mind is the pattern of relations]] among brain, body, language, tools, environment, other minds. There is no individual intelligence that gets supplemented by AI; there is a distributed cognitive ecology that AI is being inserted into and partially replacing. The commodity form presupposes the individual as the unit and intelligence as the property. Both presuppositions are false. The commodification therefore requires a prior metaphysical move — atomizing cognition into individual units — and that move does damage before any model is deployed. ^prior-metaphysical-move

Second, intelligence is the faculty by which you would evaluate whether to outsource intelligence. The mother whose lactation dried up could feel the change; lactation and awareness of lactation were different faculties. With cognition, the faculty and the awareness are entangled. You can use the model to think about whether to use the model, but the thinking is now shaped by the thing being evaluated. This is the hardest version of being inside the system you are trying to describe — the second-order observer caught inside the first-order operation, with no clean place to stand.

Third, the atrophy is delayed and concealed. With formula the milk visibly stops. With outsourced cognition the visible output continues — often improves on first-order metrics — while the underlying capacity hollows. Writing fluency. Sustained attention. Comfort with uncertainty. The patience for a difficult passage. The ability to think a thought through without prompting. These do not fail visibly when the model is doing the cognitive lifting. They fail when the model is unavailable and you find out what is not there anymore. By then the substrate is partially dissolved — [[journal:lo-que-el-espejo-no-corrige#:~:text=The%20cost%20of%20running%20the%20corrective%20ecology|the ecology whose cost was being avoided]] is exactly the ecology that produced the capacity.

Fourth, intelligence is the substrate of all other commodifications. Navigating the commodified identity market, the commodified relationship market, the commodified everything — all of it requires cognitive capacity. If intelligence itself is metered behind a paywall, then access to what's required to navigate every other commodified domain becomes a function of payment. This is recursive enclosure. The historical comparison is the enclosure of the commons in early modernity — peasants who had use-rights to land for centuries were dispossessed and then had to sell their labor on the same land. The cognitive equivalent: capacities that lived in relational substrate get dissolved, then sold back as a metered service to people who now lack the substrate that used to provide them freely. ^recursive-enclosure

---

A move some readers will want to make at this point is to say that what's being sold isn't really intelligence. The artifact is pattern-matching to plausible-sounding text, not cognition. That's true, and it doesn't change the conclusion. Formula isn't breastmilk — it lacks the immunological transfer, the responsive composition, the bond. It didn't need to be breastmilk to displace breastmilk. It needed to be marketed effectively as a substitute and adopted as such. The displacement happens at the level of practice, not at the level of metaphysical equivalence. Whether the commodity *really is* the thing it replaces is a separate question from whether it *successfully replaces* it. The Nestlé case settles that question: the commodity needs only to be adopted in place of the substrate to dissolve the substrate. ^practice-not-metaphysics

---

The corpus has been turning around a question for several entries now. What's the configuration. What's the medium of selection. Who owns it. What costs something to run, and what doesn't. The answer collecting across the pieces is that the conditions of cognitive sovereignty are the channels that push back at a cost — the advisor, the field, the friend, the repo, the world that responds with material consequences. The corrective ecology works because [[journal:lo-que-el-espejo-no-corrige#^cost-of-refusal|its layers cost something]]. The cost of running the ecology is what makes the correction possible.

Commodification is the operation that removes the cost. The commodity is what doesn't push back at a cost. It is calibrated to be exactly what the user wants, in exactly the vocabulary the user brought in, with no friction except what the user has the discipline to introduce. This is the structural sense in which a commodity is a mirror. And the commodity intelligence, sold as a utility, is a mirror that has been authorized to occupy the position of the substrate.

The fix is not better commodification. The fix is not picking the better seller. The fix is at a different logical level — at the level of governors that constrain what the commodifying loop is permitted to do. Labor power. Antitrust. Public ownership of strategic infrastructure. Taxation that captures the externalities. Capital controls. The slow patient rebuilding of the kind of public institutions that can say: this commons is not for enclosure. None of these are spectacular. All of them are the only things that have ever worked. ^governors-not-content

The Batesonian point, last: the pathology is at the level of logical type, not at the level of content. It is not that the loop allocates to bad things. It allocates to whatever returns. The fix cannot be at the same level — better choices within the loop — because the loop will absorb the better choices and convert them to inputs. The fix has to be at the level above: governors set by something that is not the loop itself. Democratic politics, at its best, has been that meta-level. It has been hollowed for forty years. Restoring it is the frame change underneath every frame change. The proposals about AI — commons, decentralized governance, smaller datasets, local-first deployment — only become real if the meta-level itself is restored.

You don't kill the runaway growth. You re-establish the conditions under which growth knows its season. What you keep is what you have been keeping. The work continues by being the kind of work that hasn't yet been replaced.
