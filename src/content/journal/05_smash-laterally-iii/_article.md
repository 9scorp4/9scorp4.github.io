On February 28, 2026, the United States and Israel launched a coordinated set of strikes against Iran, designed to do something the contest had been gestured at for two decades and never attempted: kill the leadership and end the regime. The operation was named Operation Epic Fury. The opening salvo, delivered through a combination of strikes I won't pretend to know the technical details of, included the assassination of Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, who had held the position since 1989. The intention was decapitation in the strict sense — remove the apex of the political pyramid and watch the structure collapse.

The structure did not collapse. Within hours, his son was named as successor. Within days, Iran had launched hundreds of ballistic missiles and thousands of drones at Israeli, American, and Gulf-state targets across the region. The Strait of Hormuz was effectively closed by the threat of attack on commercial vessels. By the time I am writing this — May 2026 — the ceasefire is conditional, the Strait is still effectively blockaded, gasoline in the United States costs about fifty percent more than it did in February, and the Center for Strategic and International Studies has published a report titled *Last Rounds?* about the possibility that the U.S. military is approaching what it calls Winchester — running out of ammunition for the kind of war it just decided to fight.

I find this hard to think about with any clarity, and not because the strategic logic is hard — it is in fact clearer than at any point in the previous pieces of this triptych. What is hard is what the clarity reveals. The aphorism that has been the seed of these three pieces — *if you can't smash the top, smash it laterally* — assumed an underdog using lateral against a top that could be refused. Iran, in this war, is using the same lateral logic. So is Hezbollah. So are the Houthis. So is every political entity at every scale below empire. The lateral move has stopped being an anomalous tactic deployed by the small against the large. It has become the structural condition of how everyone fights now, including those who are not refusing tops but defending them.

---

What this exposes is best named through the cybernetic frame the corpus has been holding in absentia. W. Ross Ashby published [the law of requisite variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Introduction_to_Cybernetics) in 1956. The law says, in compressed form, that the variety of a regulator must equal or exceed the variety of the system it regulates. A thermostat with two states cannot regulate a room that has more than two relevant states. [[journal:lo-que-vestia-el-poema#:%7E:text=the%20frame%20did%20not%20have%20the%20categories%20the%20grief%20was%20actually%20about|A frame with one grammar cannot carry a grief that has six]]. A government with five policies cannot regulate a society that has six kinds of problem. The law sounds banal and is in fact one of the most consequential statements in twentieth-century social science, because it sets a hard constraint on what can be regulated by what. You cannot rule a high-variety system with a low-variety regulator — [[journal:al-borde-del-fenomeno#^circuit-not-individual|es un circuito]], not an individual position. You can pretend to. You will fail. What keeps regulator and regulated aligned is the [[journal:al-borde-del-fenomeno#:%7E:text=Cognitive%20sovereignty%20is%20the%20capacity%20to%20produce%20knowledge|corrective channel]] — and when variety outstrips the channel's capacity, the channel breaks. ^requisite-variety

Empire, in the loose but useful sense the word carries, is a regulatory project. The empire-system asserts authority over a vast and diverse region, which it can only do if its regulatory variety — the combination of military force, economic power, intelligence reach, alliance density, narrative dominance, institutional legitimacy — equals or exceeds the variety of the regulated. Historically, empire has accomplished this through monopoly. The Roman legions outclassed any provincial militia. The British navy outclassed any rival fleet. The American military, after 1945, outclassed any conventional adversary by orders of magnitude. The variety asymmetry made regulation feasible.

What has changed is not the empire's variety. The American military still outclasses Iran's military by every conventional measure. What has changed is the variety of the regulated. Mass-produced cheap drones, at a few thousand dollars per unit, overwhelm air defense systems at millions of dollars per interceptor — at cost-exchange ratios that bleed the regulator's stockpiles before they bleed the regulated's. Encrypted communications and distributed networks make the surveillance apparatus's intelligence advantage local and partial rather than total. Cheap precision, in the form of guided missiles produced by middle powers, substitutes for what only empires used to be able to produce. The asymmetry has flipped. The variety of what empires now have to regulate exceeds the variety they can deploy to regulate it.

Stafford Beer worked the implications of this directly, in the most consequential cybernetic project ever attempted at state scale. Beer's [Project Cybersyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Project_Cybersyn), in Salvador Allende's Chile from 1971 to 1973, was an attempt to use real-time regulatory feedback to coordinate a socialist economy without the variety-impoverishment of central planning — [[journal:lo-que-el-libro-no-nombra#^cybersyn-up|the load-bearing flow ran *upward*]]. It was in some ways the first application of viable systems theory to a state. It was also ended by the U.S.-backed coup of September 11, 1973, in which Allende died in the presidential palace and Beer flew out of the country with what he could carry. The empire that destroyed Cybersyn was operating frontally, against a regulatory system it perceived as a threat. Half a century later, that empire is finding that the variety it once monopolized has diffused beyond its capacity to regulate. The bill on Cybersyn, paid in 1973 by Chile, has begun to come due in installments, paid by the system that imposed it.

---

The pattern is not new. It is just newly legible. The United States military has been losing structurally to lateral forces for sixty years, and the variety equation explains why each loss followed the same shape.

Vietnam: every measurable engagement won, the war lost in the United States rather than in Vietnam. ^metrics-dont-decide Iraq: every conventional engagement won, the country fragmented into sectarian militias the U.S. could not regulate, withdrawn from in 2011 with the Islamic State emerging in the gap three years later. Afghanistan: twenty years of every conventional engagement won, the Taliban back in power in 2021 weeks after the withdrawal. Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping since 2023, costing Saudi and Western air defense more per intercept than the missiles cost to fire. Hezbollah's drone campaign against Israel in 2024 and 2025. The current war with Iran is the largest live test of the pattern, but it is one in a long series. Each empire-level failure has been treated as a particular failure of execution rather than a structural condition. Each retrospective produces a new doctrine, which then fails the next time, because the doctrine remains a regulator's solution to what is actually a variety problem — [[journal:lo-que-corrige-el-mapa#^corrective-channel-definition|the corrective channel]] has been foreclosed at empire scale.

What is different about the current Iran war is the speed. Vietnam took a decade to expose the variety mismatch in a way American politics could not absorb. Iraq and Afghanistan took two decades each. The current war is producing a fuel crisis, an ammunition crisis, and an alliance crisis in the space of weeks. The acceleration is not because Iran is a more capable adversary than the Viet Cong or the Taliban. It is because variety has diffused further. Cheap drones did not exist in 1968. Encrypted distributed networks did not exist in 2003. The current empire is meeting a higher-variety world than any previous empire has met, and the response time of the variety equation is sharper accordingly.

---

The cost at empire scale arrives in different currencies than the costs at the scales of a war or a body. The U.S. is not paying primarily in soldiers' deaths in this war. It is paying in fuel inflation, in Treasury issuance, in the depletion of strategic stockpiles, in the degradation of alliance commitments, in the credibility of the deterrence frame the empire has rested on for decades. These are the costs the regulator pays for a regulatory failure that is not yet legible as a regulatory failure.

The other side pays too, of course, and more visibly. Iranian civilians have been killed in the strikes. Lebanese civilians have died in the resumed Israel-Hezbollah war. Yemenis. Palestinians. The lateral move at every scale moves cost from the actor to a population that did not choose. What is different at empire scale is that the regulator's population also pays, in deferred installments, through inflation and ammunition depletion and the slow erosion of the system's capacity to project force. The empire's tax on its own population is the slow inflation of its currency, the wear on its infrastructure, the reduction of its long-run options. The bill arrives in the form of a country that can no longer afford to be what it was, a decade or two from now, in ways its citizens experience as normal economic decline rather than as the cost of imperial overreach. The deferral pattern from the previous piece operates here too, just at the longer time horizon.

---

What makes this hard to write about cleanly is that the structural argument cuts in directions that should not be conflated. The Iranian regime is a brutal theocracy that survives partly because of its successful use of lateral leverage. The U.S. and Israeli decapitation strategy is a frontal assault that has failed because the variety equation no longer favors it. Both can be true simultaneously. The structural pattern does not tell you that the side using lateral is right, any more than the previous piece's pattern told you that Ali's refusal was right because the structure favored it.

What the structural pattern tells you is the shape of the contest, not its moral resolution. The Iranian regime's survival is structural; its theocratic character is moral and political. The U.S. empire's failure is structural; the question of what should be done with the empire's residual capacity is moral and political. Conflating the two — assuming that because lateral wins, the lateral mover is right; or that because empire fails, the empire was always wrong — is the kind of mistake that the rhetorical history of the aphorism has been encouraging since *if you can't smash the top, smash it laterally* started doing duty as a slogan rather than as an analytic. [[journal:lo-que-vestia-el-poema#^feo-y-honesto|Las dos cosas a la vez]]: ugly and honest, structural success and moral ambiguity.

This is also where the previous pieces' framings come back. [[journal:smash-laterally-i|Part I]] warned that [[journal:smash-laterally-i#:%7E:text=The%20structure%20is%20morally%20neutral|the structure was morally neutral]]; the lateral mover could be Bolshevik or Khmer Rouge or American Revolutionary or anyone willing to pay the cost. [[journal:smash-laterally-ii|Part II]] warned that [[journal:smash-laterally-ii#:%7E:text=lateral%20moves%20are%20wagers|the cost was deferred]] and that no path was free, including the path that looked like staying inside the dominant frame. Part III's complication is the universalization of these patterns. When everyone is doing lateral and everyone is paying deferred, the moral question — *for what?* — becomes the only thing that distinguishes one structural success from another. The structure has stopped doing the moral work. It never was doing it. It just looked like it was when the underdogs we sympathized with were the ones using it. ^everyone-lateral

---

There is something I find clarifying about thinking through this carefully, even though the resolution it points toward is uncomfortable. The aphorism that started this triptych was useful as a critique of frontal force, of cleverness without weight, of the assumption that brute approach beats strategic indirection. It is still useful in that capacity, at smaller scales. The bicycles in the jungle are still real. Ali's refusal in Houston is still real. The lateral move at the scale of a war or one body is still operative as a frame and still demands the cost-recognition the previous pieces were trying to make unavoidable.

What I don't think is still operative is the aphorism's underlying assumption — that there is a top to refuse, a frame to subvert, a contest whose terms one can decline by playing on different terms. At the scale where the empire-system meets the high-variety world, the top has dissolved into the regulatory condition that everyone is now operating inside. The Iranian regime is using lateral. The U.S. empire is finding that it cannot regulate against lateral with the variety it has. The Houthis are using lateral. The Ukraine war is fought through drones produced for hundreds of dollars by mid-sized industrial economies. The contest has flattened into a shared structural condition in which everyone is below an apex that no longer exists, refusing a frame that has dispersed into the conditions of everyone's operation.

The triptych has been about the cost of a slogan that hides what it costs. The first piece argued that the lateral move relocates cost; the second that it defers cost; the third that at the largest scale, the cost is paid by the system that hosts everyone, including those who thought they were outside it. Three versions of one argument. The lateral move is real. The cost is real. The slogan is partial because slogans are partial.

What the third version adds, that the first two could not say from inside their scales, is that there is no longer a stable position from which to admire the lateral move from outside. The reader [[journal:smash-laterally-i#:%7E:text=admire%20the%20cleverness%20of%20the%20lateral%20move%20and%20underweight%20the%20cost|admiring the lateral move]] at empire scale is admiring it from inside an empire whose regulatory condition is failing. The lateral move's underdog romance was always the romance of an observer who was on the side that did not yet need to use it. When everyone is lateral, the observer position dissolves — [[journal:el-lazo-que-cierra-los-lazos#^entropy-budget|the limiting factor has disappeared]]. There is [[journal:una-palabra-varias-aguas#^edge-information|nowhere from which to keep score]]. [[journal:lo-que-vestia-el-poema#^what-is-love|The question I will keep open]] — at intimate scale, *what is love*; at empire scale, *for what?* — outlasts the diagnostic. ^no-outside

The map has to be paid for in territory, the first piece said. The receipt is in a currency the contest didn't print, the second said.

The territory has eaten the map.
