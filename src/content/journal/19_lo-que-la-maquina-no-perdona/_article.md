A 1950s Plymouth Fury runs on a Soviet engine and a Japanese gearbox. A washing-machine motor is rewired into a fan; the same model of motor, in another house, drives a table saw. An orange squeezer was cast from melted aluminum, a television antenna folded out of a food tray. None of these objects is being used for the thing it was built to do. The Cuban designer Ernesto Oroza, who spent years [collecting them across the island](https://www.ernestooroza.com/), calls the ethos behind them *desobediencia tecnológica* — technological disobedience — and summarizes it as a refusal to respect the authority of objects: to treat the designed purpose of a thing as raw material rather than as a command.

The phrase carries more than it advertises. The designed purpose of an object is a map. It is a prescription, frozen into the thing at the factory, of how the object is to be used and what counts as using it correctly. That map was drawn somewhere else — in an industrial economy with a parts supply, a service network, a replacement cycle. *Resolver*, the Cuban verb for getting a thing done with whatever is at hand, is what happens when the territory stops matching the map. The motor does not care that it was a washing machine. The territory — heat, no replacement fan, a coil that still turns — overrules the prescription. This is [[journal:lo-que-corrige-el-mapa#^corrective-channel-definition|the map being corrected by the territory]] in its most literal form: not a figure for how knowledge works, but a motor turning air it was never meant to move.

---

Here is where the story should, by this garden's own earlier arguments, go wrong.

Two prior entries claimed that a system cut off from external correction drifts. A model trained on its own outputs loses contact with what it is about and [[journal:lo-que-corrige-el-mapa#^model-collapse-bateson|confabulates while preserving internal coherence]] — the map eats the territory and starts copying itself. The Special Period severed precisely that kind of channel. The U.S. embargo and the collapse of Soviet support cut the island off from the parts supply, the replacement market, the external source that, in a working economy, corrects a broken machine from outside. By the logic traced earlier, Cuba's machine ecology should have spiraled into junk — improvisation stacked on improvisation, each fix one step further from any working standard, the whole thing drifting away from function while feeling, from inside, like competence.

It didn't. *Resolver* produced durable, genuinely novel solutions that kept machines alive for decades. So the earlier argument was too coarse, and this is the correction worth making: closure as such is not what causes the drift. Losing contact with the *territory* is.

The resolver's loop is closed with respect to *supply* — no new parts come in. It is violently open with respect to *material consequence*. The fan spins or it doesn't. The rewired motor runs or it burns out in the hand holding it. There is no interval in which a bad repair passes for a good one; the machine refuses the false fix immediately and without appeal. What collapses a self-referential system is not the absence of an external supplier — it is the absence of the territory, and the resolver never loses the territory, because the territory is the thing he is holding while it gets hot. Model collapse happens because a model is [[journal:lo-que-el-sello-no-certifica#^training-circularity|trained on inscriptions, not on the territory the inscriptions referred to]]. The resolver is trained on nothing but territory. He is the photonegative of model collapse: maximally cut off from resupply, minimally drifting, because the single corrective channel that matters — material consequence, the layer that does not negotiate — is exactly the one scarcity left intact.

---

This reframes the strangest part of the phenomenon: that the state encouraged it.

A centrally planned economy is, in cybernetic terms, a regulator trying to absorb the variety of everything that can go wrong across a country. Every machine fails in its own way; every part becomes unavailable on its own schedule; the failures do not arrive in a form a ministry in Havana can see, let alone match. Only variety absorbs variety. The center does not have enough of it. It cannot hold the requisite variety to repair a nation's machines from the middle.

So it devolved the variety to the edge. In the 1970s a group of mechanics and engineers formed the National Association of Innovators and Rationalizers — ANIR — to organize this homebrew ingenuity, binding the tinkerer's ethos to the needs of an isolated economy. In 1992, at the depth of the Special Period, the Cuban military issued *[Con Nuestros Propios Esfuerzos](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/277326650_Worker_build_your_own_machinery_A_workshop_to_practice_the_Technological_Disobedience)* — "With Our Own Efforts" — a book of crowdsourced methods for manipulating, repairing, and repurposing everyday objects. The deepest root is older still: a 1961 speech in which Che Guevara, then minister of industry, exhorted workers to self-produce the spare parts the country could not import — remembered by its slogan, *¡obrero, construye tu maquinaria!*

Read against [[journal:lo-que-el-sello-no-certifica#^subsidy-of-restraint|the worry about inscription]], the manual is a peculiar object. That earlier worry was about inscription running *ahead* of the territory — records that launder a moment's affect into permanent fact, structurally deaf to whatever they were supposed to be about. *Con Nuestros Propios Esfuerzos* runs the other way. It is inscription *downstream* of the territory: it did not prescribe the fixes, it transcribed the ones that had already survived the machine. The state did not author the corrective ecology; it wrote down what the corrective ecology had already proven. For once the seal followed the territory instead of replacing it — the center formally licensing a variety it could not itself generate, signing its name beneath solutions already tested by something that does not forgive a wrong answer.

---

It is tempting to close on triumph: the spirit overcoming scarcity, necessity mothering invention, the island of hackers. Oroza's collection invites the reading, and [the documentaries](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/al-jazeera-world/2026/2/25/cuba-technical-disobedience) lean into it.

The reading is available mostly to whoever is not the one whose fan stopped. *Resolver* is not a philosophy of liberation from the tyranny of objects. It is what the territory extracts when the map is withdrawn and there is no choice but to answer. The motor driving the table saw is, by every account, dangerous. The repair is temporary; the machine will fail again; the person fixing it is tired. The authority of the object is cheap to disrespect in an essay and expensive to disrespect with your hands. What looks from outside like inventive freedom is, from inside, [[journal:lo-que-el-espejo-no-corrige#^cost-of-refusal|the cost of refusal]] landing on the one person in the loop who cannot refuse — because the heat does not wait for him to feel inspired.
